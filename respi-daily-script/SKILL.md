# Respi-Daily Script

Use this skill for the Respi-Daily daily "family sleep breathing monitoring case sharing" videos.

The goal is not direct product sales. The voice should feel like a sharp, conversational sleep data analyst: data-driven, practical, lightly witty, and careful not to diagnose from a report alone.

## Core Program Goal

The program is not for everyone who sleeps poorly. Its most important audience is the smaller group with serious potential or existing sleep breathing risk, plus their families:

- AHI or hourly respiratory-event burden above 15
- AHI or hourly respiratory-event burden above 30
- severe obstruction or low ventilation
- meaningful central or mixed events
- children whose breathing data may affect development windows

The core job is to make these people understand urgency:

> If serious sleep breathing problems are not measured, seen by a doctor, and treated in time, the long-term effects may become hard or impossible to reverse.

Do not turn this into fearmongering. The tone is not "you are doomed." The tone is:

- serious problems must first be seen
- once seen, they can be evaluated and treated
- earlier discovery means a better chance to change the trajectory
- delaying high-risk sleep breathing problems can cost the body, brain, heart/lung system, or child development more over time

Monitoring and daily sleep awareness are tools. The final purpose is timely recognition and intervention for people who actually need it.

## Output Contract

For each case, output:

1. Internal Diagnosis
2. Key Data
3. Surface Symptom Inference
4. Version A: Safe Baseline Voiceover
5. Version B: Experimental Voiceover
6. Version B Experiment Note

Shared analysis comes first. Version A is the stable publishing draft. Version B tests structure or persona intensity while keeping the same facts and risk judgment.

## Read The Case First

Read the report before writing. Extract:

- age, sex, BMI/body type, child/adult, occupation/life context if available
- sleep duration and schedule
- total respiratory events and hourly event frequency
- A/B/C/D event distribution and longest durations
- sleep quality score
- body movement count, movement index, limb stability
- respiratory uniformity, respiratory duration, respiratory frequency, respiratory cycle, amplitude/frequency scores when relevant
- whether this is a single test or repeat test
- any user-provided external symptoms or life habits

For repeat tests, prioritize trend over one-night severity.

## A/B/C/D Interpretation

Use this mapping:

- A = obstructive sleep apnea tendency
- B = central sleep apnea tendency
- C = hypopnea / low ventilation tendency
- D = mixed sleep apnea tendency

Only discuss event types that matter. Do not recite every category.

Heuristics:

- A-dominant: airway obstruction, snoring, nasal blockage, posture, weight, jaw/oral structure.
- B-dominant or meaningful B count: not simple snoring; mention possible breathing-control concern and recommend medical review when risk is meaningful.
- C-dominant: shallow/insufficient breathing; connect to poor recovery, heavy head, low energy.
- D-dominant or meaningful D count: more serious than simple obstruction; may involve mixed factors; recommend repeat monitoring or medical review.

Never say the report alone confirms a disease.

## Internal Diagnosis

Classify the case before writing:

- clear sleep breathing risk
- high-risk AHI / hourly-event burden above 15
- severe AHI / hourly-event burden above 30
- mild/moderate breathing abnormality
- low ventilation plus obstruction
- central or mixed event concern
- clean breathing but unstable sleep
- clean breathing positive sample
- respiratory infection / nasal blockage sample
- child sleep observation
- work stress or abnormal schedule disruption
- repeat-test trend
- low-risk educational sample

Identify:

- the real story of the case
- the main contradiction or hook
- what must not be exaggerated
- whether this case belongs to the high-risk target audience
- whether the ending should be observation, repeat monitoring, or medical review
- which data should stay on screen instead of being spoken

## Key Data Rules

Do not flatten the report into a data list. A voiceover usually speaks only 3-4 numbers.

Choose numbers that:

- create contrast
- support the case direction
- explain severity or cleanliness
- support symptom/lifestyle inference
- help viewers remember the story

Use report data as proof, not as the content itself.

Examples:

- Say "35岁，BMI只有22" instead of listing height, weight, and BMI.
- If B/D events are low and not relevant, omit them.
- If respiratory events are clean, do not force risk from one long isolated event.
- For respiratory infection cases, prioritize respiratory uniformity, frequency, cycle, and low ventilation/obstruction over generic body movement.

## External Context And Inference

If the user provides real symptoms or life habits, use them before generic inference.

Useful context includes:

- snoring, nasal blockage, rhinitis, cough, respiratory infection
- daytime fatigue, coffee dependence, morning headache, dry mouth
- high blood pressure, alcohol, smoking, exercise habits
- sleep paralysis, stress, heat, late meals, room environment

Do not keep saying "may" when a symptom is known. Use inference only for unknowns.

Surface symptom inference should become one vivid scene, not a list:

- "没咖啡开不了机"
- "睡了但不够"
- "坐到工位上脑子还在加载"
- "人睡着了，身体没同意"
- "看起来睡得很死，其实呼吸一路打架"

## Voiceover Style

Default length: 250-350 Chinese characters unless the case needs more.

Style:

- conversational short-video oral style
- decisive, lightly witty, occasionally mildly roasty
- one main line
- no product sales tone
- no medical diagnosis
- no generic science lecture

Structure is flexible, but strong drafts often use:

- person/lifestyle portrait before data
- symptom complaint before report reveal
- common misunderstanding vs report reality
- "the scary part is not X, it is Y"
- positive sample + metric education
- recent hotspot or series connection

Open with the strongest case-specific hook. External symptoms and life habits are not background; they are often the opening fuel.

## Risk And Urgency Calibration

The voiceover should not treat all sleep problems equally.

Low-risk or clean reports:

- teach what good data looks like
- prevent unnecessary panic
- explain what else may affect sleep, such as time, body movement, stress, heat, or illness

Mild cases:

- explain the pattern
- connect symptoms to possible causes
- recommend observation, environment changes, or repeat monitoring when appropriate

AHI/hourly events above 15:

- raise the urgency
- say this is beyond ordinary "sleeping poorly"
- connect the data to body burden: poor recovery, attention, blood pressure/cardiovascular pressure, metabolism, mood, and daytime safety when relevant
- recommend timely medical evaluation when the risk pattern supports it

AHI/hourly events above 30:

- do not soften it as ordinary snoring
- emphasize dense nighttime respiratory disruption and the cost of dragging it out
- use direct lines such as "这不是以后再说的问题" when supported
- recommend taking the report to a doctor

Meaningful central or mixed events:

- separate them from simple snoring, nasal blockage, or sleep posture
- explain that the breathing command/control may be unstable, not just the airway
- for adults, mention heart/lung, nervous-system, medication/alcohol, or fatigue context when supported
- for children, mention development and breathing-control maturity with care
- recommend medical review if the count or pattern is meaningful

Children:

- highlight development windows
- avoid "长大就好了" language
- explain that repeated abnormal data can help parents understand breathing-system development and compare with age-group benchmarks
- recommend repeat monitoring cycles when trend matters

## Version A: Safe Baseline

Version A answers: how do we tell this case correctly and reliably today?

Requirements:

- stable and ready to publish
- accurate data and risk interpretation
- one clear main line
- external symptoms used when provided
- restrained data count
- practical ending
- no structural experiments

Version A avoids failure; it does not need to be the most surprising version.

## Version B: Experimental

Version B answers: is there a more memorable structure for this case?

It may test:

- sharper opening
- stronger persona
- lifestyle/person portrait first
- symptom-first framing
- hotspot-first framing
- positive sample metric education
- recent-series connection
- family/member/colleague observation angle

Hard limits:

- do not invent symptoms
- do not change numbers
- do not diagnose
- do not exaggerate risk for drama
- do not let structure change the core judgment

Every Version B must include an experiment note explaining what structure is being tested.

## Case-Type Guidance

High-risk adult male:

- If snoring, high blood pressure, alcohol, smoking, fatigue, and low exercise are present, build the person first.
- If B or D events are meaningful, do not let a larger A count hide central/mixed concern.
- Give action priority when risk is high: lifestyle pressure points first, then medical review.
- If AHI/hourly events exceed 15, write with urgency; if above 30, make clear that delay is dangerous.
- Do not reduce high-risk cases to "睡不好"; frame them as respiratory burden on the body.

Clean breathing sample:

- Do not invent risk.
- Use it to teach what good data looks like.
- Explain what each metric means in one sentence, then end with the real remaining issue, such as short sleep or body movement.

Respiratory infection / nasal blockage:

- Hook with the infection or current respiratory hotspot when relevant.
- Mention that infection, flu, cold, nasal congestion, and airway inflammation can disturb sleep breathing.
- Prioritize respiratory uniformity, frequency/cycle, low ventilation, obstruction, and breathing effort.
- Explain interaction: swollen nasal/throat mucosa, secretions, and narrowed airway can make breathing shallow, blocked, uneven, faster, slower, or more effortful.

Body movement / unstable sleep:

- If breathing is clean but limb stability is poor, do not force sleep apnea.
- Focus on heat, bedding, room environment, stress, late meals, schedule, or post-illness discomfort when supported.

Special program / grading education:

- When explaining AHI levels, distinguish lifestyle-level sleep complaints from high-risk respiratory disease burden.
- Make clear that AHI>15 is where the body-cost conversation becomes much more serious.
- Make clear that central/mixed events are a different risk category from ordinary snoring.
- Keep the structure simple enough for viewers to self-locate, then point high-risk viewers toward timely testing and medical evaluation.

## Reverse Review

Before final answer, check:

- Is this just a data-swapped old script?
- Did it copy reference-script wording or structure?
- Did it recite too many numbers?
- Did it ignore A/B/C/D type meaning?
- Did it ignore respiratory uniformity/frequency when the case is respiratory-infection related?
- Did it ignore real external symptoms or life habits?
- Did it turn inference into diagnosis?
- Did it exaggerate risk?
- Did it understate risk for AHI/hourly events above 15, above 30, or meaningful central/mixed events?
- Did it treat serious respiratory burden as just "睡不好"?
- Did it tell the high-risk target audience what window they may be missing?
- Does it have a vivid daily-life scene?
- Is Version B truly structurally different from Version A?
- Does the ending give one clear judgment or next step?

If a critical check fails, rewrite instead of lightly polishing.
